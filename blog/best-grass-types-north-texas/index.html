Sod Installation Guide: The Best Grass Types for North Texas Lawns

Why the Right Grass Matters in North Texas

North Texas is not an easy place to grow a lawn: 100-degree summer stretches, soil from heavy black clay in Dallas to sandy loam further west, rainfall swinging from drought to flood, and surprise ice storms. The grass you choose has to handle all of it, matched to your sun, foot traffic, water budget, and maintenance appetite.

Bermuda: The North Texas Standard

Bermuda is the most common turf grass in North Texas for good reason: exceptional drought tolerance once established, fast recovery from damage, and the best foot-traffic handling of any warm-season grass. Popular varieties: TifTuf (drought resistance, fine texture), Celebration (deep green, better shade tolerance for a Bermuda), and Common (budget-friendly for large areas).

The catch: Bermuda needs full sun and goes dormant brown from roughly November through March. Overseeding with ryegrass keeps winter color but adds maintenance.

St. Augustine: Best for Shade

If mature trees shade your lawn, St. Augustine wins. It thrives on as little as four to six hours of direct sun and produces a thick, tropical-looking lawn. Palmetto offers good cold tolerance; Raleigh has been a North Texas staple for decades. Trade-offs: higher water needs, slower recovery, chinch bug susceptibility, and weaker traffic tolerance than Bermuda.

Zoysia: The Premium Middle Ground

Zoysia sits between the other two on nearly every measure, more shade-tolerant than Bermuda and more drought-tolerant than St. Augustine, with a dense, carpet-like feel. Zeon Zoysia is the premium pick: fine texture, excellent drought resistance, and color that holds into fall. It costs 20 to 30 percent more than Bermuda and grows slowly, so bare spots fill in gradually.

What to Expect During Installation

Proper soil preparation decides whether a new lawn succeeds. The ground is cleared of old grass and weeds, tilled four to six inches deep, amended (Dallas clay benefits from compost or topsoil), and graded away from the foundation. Sod goes down in a staggered brick pattern with tight seams, followed by a heavy initial watering and a strict two-to-three-week establishment schedule.
